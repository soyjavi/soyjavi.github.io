    1. 01 / Early years · 1980–1986

      Early years.

      From birth to the day I turned seven.

      Apr 1980

      I was born before dawn in a big hospital

      I was born one April morning before dawn, around one o'clock, in a big hospital on the outskirts. My mother was the eldest daughter of a family that had emigrated from the coast to make a living in the north, and my father was a metalworker from a coastal town.

      Both were very young and came from simple families. My maternal and paternal grandparents are there too, at the start of this story. I remember nothing, of course; I only know what I have been told, and I keep it as the first line of everything else.

    2. 01 / Early years · 1980–1986

      ~1980

      I was born in a house with cows around it

      I spent my first two or three years in my father's family farmhouse, not in a city. It was a big house shared with several generations, up to a great-great-grandfather, and there were cows around it.

      My parents were very young and still had no money to buy a home of their own, so we started there, in a humble family, lower-middle class, as I call it myself. I do not remember those years in detail, but I know that was my starting point: a borrowed country house, with old people inside and animals outside.

      When I was two or three, my parents bought their first home and we moved from the farmhouse to a quiet, half-working-class neighbourhood. It was a fifth floor with no lift, bought outright, and for them that was a great deal.

      Soda from my grandfather's glass

      I must have been five or six. On Sundays we ate at my grandparents' plot of land, and my grandfather put the radio on. He let us try a little of his soda, and to me that was a promotion: drinking from his glass was, in a small boy's head, something grown-ups did.

      Summers in the campsite

      In summer we went camping, and in winter my father took us up to the hills at weekends. It is the childhood I sum up in one word: normal. I remember one of those campsites above all, next to a canal, enormous in my head, and I remember playing with whatever children turned up there. More than the places, what I keep is the texture: the whole family outside with very little, and the feeling that everything was fine.

    10. 01 / Early years · 1980–1986

      ~1986

      I date my childhood by school years, not by years

      When I talk about my childhood I do not give years, I give school years. I did the whole old system: eight compulsory years, from six to fourteen, and from there you went on to the baccalaureate or to vocational training.

      That is why I remember things by the year I was in and not by the date. It was the system I happened to live through, and I am one of the last to come out of it: the law that changed it arrived just as I was finishing. When I want to order my memories, the first thing I do is put them in their school year.

    11. 01 / Early years · 1980–1986

      ~1986

      The grid I use to order my school years

      When I had to put dates to my childhood, I made a simple list: the eight years of the old school, one after another, with the year each one fell in. The first, the 1986-87 school year, and so on up to the eighth, at fourteen.

      With that grid I date every school episode I remember, because I name them by the year I was in and not by the calendar year. It is a way of not getting lost: every memory has its box, and that way I know where I was when it happened.

    1. 02 / School years · 1988–1993

      School years.

      From seven to fourteen.

      1988

      The family sport

      I started at eight, following a tradition at home: my father and my uncle had both trained in it when they were young, so taekwondo was more or less the family sport. I went as far as the brown belt, at fourteen, and for all those years I was always the tallest in the class.

      What stayed with me is not the gym but the fact that it never stopped. When life broke and we went far away, I kept training wherever there was room: with my mother as my teacher, sometimes on the beach. She is the reason I did not quit when there was no mat and no coach.

    2. 02 / School years · 1988–1993

      ~Mar 1988

      The night the house broke

      I was eight and I was ill at my grandparents' house. I got up in the night, was sick, and went back to bed. I saw nothing: I woke up to shouting. Someone was asking who had done it, and at eight years old I was not able to say it was me. They all knew it was me, and I stayed silent. That silence lit a fight between my parents, and out of it came a separation. We went far away, to my other grandparents' house, and I carried the whole guilt with me: I was sure it had been my fault. Suddenly the world was too big for an eight-year-old boy. I am not sure we ever talked about that night.

      ~1988

      The small television, the toys and the last carnival

      We were no different from anyone: we watched television at home and played with whatever there was. What I remember most is the programme about the sea that I watched with my parents. While it was on they put my father's motorbike goggles on me as if they were a diving helmet, and I looked at the bottom of the ocean from a fifth floor with no lift.

With my brother I watched the wrestling and we had figures of those fighters. And I remember the carnival when I dressed up as a cartoon character: it was the last one the four of us spent together, because weeks later we left.

    4. 02 / School years · 1988–1993

      ~1988

      We were the Basques

      I arrived in the village at seven, with the family freshly broken. There everyone spoke Galician and we were the Basques: by accent and by origin, we did not fit. People asked me whether we were not afraid where we came from, that bombs could go off.

      In all that time I never set foot in a school: my mother taught us with the usual workbooks, and the taekwondo went on, sometimes on the beach. I made my first communion there, a bit unofficial and in a borrowed suit. And yes, I mix up the nineties with what was in fact 1988: that is how I remember it.

    7. 02 / School years · 1988–1993

      1992

      Three months at home in plaster

      I tore the vastus medialis trying to jump a wall in a park that was not ours. I was limping and it hurt, but I kept playing, walked home and said nothing. My parents asked about the limp and I played it down. The next day I could not move my leg.

      At the hospital they gave us two options: surgery or a plaster up to the groin. My father chose the plaster. I spent three months without leaving the house, on a fifth floor with no lift, with morning television for company; from that term I remember one classmate who came to tell me what we had done in class. When the plaster came off, the leg had lost its strength, and that was the year I ate at the school canteen.

      My teeth flying in the kitchen

      I must have been thirteen or fourteen, and I was messing about in the kitchen at home when I fell and broke both front teeth. I was wearing my taekwondo uniform. It was the second country I ever set foot in, and the first one I did not visit for pleasure: I saw it with my mouth open. Years later I am still thirteen in that kitchen.

    5. 04 / Building a life · 2005–2020

      ~2009

      An API, a front end and a message

      It started with a chance find: someone had built, on his own, an API and an app to check the bus timetables of the province. I had a different phone, so his app was no use to me, but his service was. I built my own front end on top of it with a framework of my own, in HTML5, and wrote to him to show him.

      Everything came from there. I brought the idea, open-source multiplatform software, and the product. I told my brother afterwards, and he ended up sitting down with us: he handled the money, which is what he was studying. In October 2011 the three of us founded the company with equal shares. No market study, no business plan: an API, a front end built at home and a message.
